Price Signals, episode 91: Why people stopped saving

HOST (June Albright): My guest is Marcus Bell, chief economist at Harbor County Credit Union. Marcus, you looked at your members' savings over the last few years. What changed?

MARCUS: The average savings rate among our members fell from 11.2% of income to 6.4%. The drop started right after deposit rates were cut close to zero, and it hasn't recovered.

JUNE: An Austrian economist would say that's time preference at work.

MARCUS: That's how I read it too. Interest is the price of waiting. When the reward for waiting goes to almost nothing, people rationally choose to consume now. Our members didn't become less disciplined overnight. The signal telling them to save got switched off.

JUNE: Who was hit hardest?

MARCUS: Younger members. Members under 30 now hold median savings of $1,900. That's less than one bad month for a lot of them: a car repair and a medical bill and they're into credit card debt.

JUNE: Did anything you tried actually work?

MARCUS: One thing did. We launched a round-up program, where every debit card purchase rounds up to the next dollar and the difference goes into savings. Members who enrolled increased their savings 14% over the following year compared with similar members who didn't.

JUNE: Why does that work when a higher rate would be the textbook fix?

MARCUS: Because it doesn't ask for a decision. Most people don't fail to save because they decided not to. They fail because saving requires choosing, again and again, to not spend money that's sitting right there.

JUNE: What would you tell a listener who wants to save more?

MARCUS: Set up an automatic transfer to savings on payday, before the money ever hits your checking balance. Whatever you can see, you'll spend. Whatever moves before you see it, you mostly won't miss.

JUNE: Marcus Bell, thank you.
