Sound Money Hour, episode 221: Inflation at the grocery store

HOST (Luis Ferreira): My guest is Hannah Liu, who built the Pacific Basket Index. Hannah, what is it?

HANNAH: It's a price tracker I started as a side project. Every month, volunteers record the shelf price and package size of the same 140 grocery items at stores across three West Coast states.

LUIS: What have you found?

HANNAH: Over three years, the total price of the basket rose 23%. That's noticeably higher than the official food inflation figure for the same period, and I think I know part of the reason.

LUIS: Which is?

HANNAH: Shrinkflation. Of our 140 items, 31 got smaller without getting cheaper. The cereal box is the same height but thinner. The chip bag has more air. If you only track the sticker price, you miss all of that.

LUIS: What was the single biggest price increase?

HANNAH: Olive oil, up 61% over the three years. Some of that is crop failures in Europe, but it was a shock to people who cook with it every day.

LUIS: Why does this matter beyond the grocery bill?

HANNAH: Because prices are information. When they change quietly, through smaller packages, people lose the ability to see what's happening to the value of their money. They feel poorer and can't point to why.

LUIS: What should a listener actually do with this?

HANNAH: Start tracking unit prices instead of sticker prices. Most shelf tags show the price per ounce or per count in small print. That's the number that tells you the truth. If you compare unit prices, shrinkflation can't hide from you.

LUIS: Hannah Liu, thank you.
