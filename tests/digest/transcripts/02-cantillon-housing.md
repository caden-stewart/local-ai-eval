Price Signals, episode 88: Who gets the new money first?

HOST (June Albright): Today I'm talking with Dr. Elena Voss, an economist who spent two years studying credit expansion in Marlow County. Elena, start with the setup.

ELENA: Over a four-year period, regional banks in Marlow County expanded mortgage and construction lending by $2.3 billion. That's a large number for a county of about half a million people. The question I cared about was not whether prices rose, because of course they did. It was where they rose first, and who paid for it.

JUNE: This is the Cantillon effect.

ELENA: Right. Richard Cantillon noticed centuries ago that new money doesn't arrive everywhere at once. It enters through specific doors, and the people standing closest to those doors spend it before prices adjust. In Marlow County the doors were bank branches and the developers who borrowed from them.

JUNE: What did the data show?

ELENA: Home prices in the neighborhoods within two miles of the busiest lending branches rose 18% over the period. In the outer towns, farther from where the credit was being created, prices rose 7%. Same county, same interest rates, very different outcomes.

JUNE: And wages?

ELENA: Wages across the county grew 3.1% over the same stretch. So for a household that depends on a paycheck rather than on borrowing, housing near the core became dramatically less affordable.

JUNE: Who got squeezed out?

ELENA: First-time buyers. Their share of home purchases in the county fell from 34% to 22%. The buyers who replaced them were mostly investors and people who already owned property they could borrow against.

JUNE: Some people would say that's just a hot housing market.

ELENA: A hot market doesn't explain the geography. If it were only demand from people moving in, you wouldn't see the price gains cluster so tightly around where the lending happened.

JUNE: What would you change?

ELENA: I'm not proposing a lending ban. My recommendation is modest: banks should publish their lending by zip code every quarter. Right now nobody outside the banks can see where the new credit is going until years later, when researchers like me dig it out. Transparency lets people see the doors.

JUNE: Elena Voss, thank you.
